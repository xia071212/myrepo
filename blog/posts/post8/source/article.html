How Can One Sample Tell Us About an Entire Population?

Understanding statistical inference through interactive simulation

A city economist cannot interview every household before estimating monthly spending. Statistical inference measures uncertainty across samples. Here, you can draw households, build confidence intervals, and test a spending claim in a city whose true mean we know.

Why do samples differ?

Figure 1 contains 20,000 simulated households, generated from a right-skewed lognormal distribution and rescaled to your chosen mean, μ. These are invented dollar amounts, not survey evidence. We sample without replacement: households have equal selection chances, and none appears twice within a survey. Figure 2 shows those selected observations on the same horizontal scale.

Click “Draw another sample”: the sample mean moves; the population stays fixed. Increasing sample size usually brings the estimate closer to μ, although individual estimates still vary. The spread of household spending is different from the uncertainty of its estimated average: the latter generally shrinks as more households are surveyed.

How certain is the estimate?

Bootstrap estimates sampling uncertainty from one observed sample when the population distribution is unknown. Treat the sample as the population: draw n observations with replacement, calculate their mean, and repeat B times. Figure 3 plots these means; their spread approximates the sample mean’s variability across fresh samples.

R is used only in Figure 4 to check coverage: draw R fresh samples, each with n households, and build one interval per sample using B resamples. It is not needed to construct the single interval in Figure 3. Increasing R makes the coverage estimate more stable; it does not improve that single interval.

{{bootstrap}}

A 95% percentile confidence interval runs from the 2.5th percentile (2.5% of bootstrap means lie below it) to the 97.5th percentile (97.5% lie below it). The highlighted band keeps the central 95% of bootstrap means; the solid line marks the true mean μ. Raise confidence to retain more of the distribution and widen the interval. Increase B to stabilize its estimated endpoints; this does not add households or remove uncertainty caused by a small original sample.

{{coverage}}

Figure 4 asks whether this procedure works across repeated surveys. Each experiment draws an independent population sample, bootstraps that sample, and constructs one interval. The vertical line marks μ; orange intervals miss it. Coverage is the fraction of all R intervals containing μ; the plot shows the first 100. With the default settings (μ = $3,000, n = 150, B = 1,200, 95% confidence, R = 1,000, seed = 20261101), {{covered}} intervals cover μ: {{coverage_pct}}%, close to 95%.

95% confidence means that if we repeatedly drew independent samples from the same population and constructed an interval each time using this method, approximately 95% of those intervals would contain the fixed true mean μ. The intervals vary across samples; μ does not. It does not mean a 95% probability that μ lies in the particular interval already calculated.

Is $3,000 a plausible mean?

A test statistic summarizes how far the sample result is from a claim, relative to sampling uncertainty. Test H₀: μ = μ₀ against H₁: μ ≠ μ₀, initially μ₀ = $3,000. Here, t = (x̄ − μ₀)/(s/√n): x̄ is the sample mean and s its standard deviation. The estimated standard error, s/√n, measures typical variation in sample means. Thus, t expresses the observed gap in standard-error units.

{{null}}

Figure 5 simulates samples under H₀, rescaling the population to μ₀ and recomputing t. Rejection regions are the statistic values for which we reject H₀, shown as orange tails. The significance level α is the chosen risk of rejecting H₀ when it is actually true (a Type I error). At α = 5%, about 5% of null statistics enter these regions. Reject if the observed line enters either tail. Such outcomes are rare under H₀, providing evidence against it.

{{pvalue}}

Figures 5 and 6 use the same null distribution: Figure 5 sets rejection thresholds using α; Figure 6 measures extremeness using the observed |t|. Its orange tails show simulated outcomes at least this extreme in either direction. This area estimates the p-value: the probability, assuming H₀ and the model are true, of a statistic at least this extreme. A small total orange area means our result is unusual under H₀. Reject when p ≤ α; change μ₀ or α to explore the decision. The p-value is not the probability that H₀ is true; failure to reject does not establish equality.

What one sample can tell us

Bootstrap estimates uncertainty from one sample: resample with replacement, plot the means, and use their 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles for a 95% interval. The default interval is {{ci_dollars}}. 95% confidence describes repeated-sample coverage: approximately 95% of intervals should contain μ; here, {{covered}}/1,000 do. Hypothesis testing assesses evidence against a claim by comparing the observed statistic with simulations under H₀. Rejection regions contain values deemed too extreme at the chosen α. The p-value is the probability under H₀ and the model of a statistic at least as extreme. Here, p = {{pvalue_number}} exceeds 0.05, so we do not reject the $3,000 claim.
