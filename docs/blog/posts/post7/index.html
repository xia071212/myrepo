Closer to AI, Easier to Replace?

Artificial Intelligence
Employment
Interactive Plotly
Does a closer connection to AI mean a greater risk of job replacement?
Author

Yuting Xia

Published

October 26, 2026

If AI can perform more of a job’s tasks, it seems natural to expect fewer workers to be needed. Yet AI might also help workers produce more, making their skills more valuable. So this article explores whether a job’s closer connection to AI means a greater risk of replacement.

How do we measure the connection to AI?

Anthropic’s Economic Index maps observed Claude usage to work tasks and occupations. The BLS Employment Projections database provides U.S. employment by occupation in 2024 and projected employment in 2034. Python joins them using Standard Occupational Classification (SOC) codes; an occupation such as accounting can span many industries.

AI exposure summarizes observed AI use across occupational tasks, accounting for factors such as task time and usage patterns. Task breadth is the share of matched tasks with positive AI penetration, counting each task equally. Neither measures the probability that a worker will lose their job.

The matched sample contains 722 occupations, retaining those with at least 95% task matching against O*NET 30.0 task descriptions. The vertical axis measures projected employment growth. It provides an employment outlook, not an observed AI replacement rate.

Explore: hover for employment and sample coverage; click a group to see its occupations. Search for one occupation to isolate it, then clear the search to restore the group. Switch the horizontal axis to compare exposure with task breadth.

Group exposure and breadth use 2024 employment weights. Bubble area represents sample employment; group growth uses summed employment. Hover shows sample coverage relative to the official group. Detailed dots are equal-sized so smaller occupations remain visible.

Does closer AI exposure mean more replacement?

Similar AI exposure can accompany very different employment outlooks. In the group view, compare business and financial operations with sales: their exposure is almost the same, at 28.8% and 27.5%, yet projected employment grows 5.2% in business/finance and falls 2.6% in sales. Their bubbles sit close together horizontally but on opposite sides of zero growth.

Computer and mathematical occupations provide a second comparison. Their exposure is 35.3%, slightly higher than the 33.9% for office and administrative support. Yet computer/math employment is projected to grow 10.0%, while office support declines 3.9%. The more exposed group has the stronger outlook, which challenges the simple expectation that closer contact with AI necessarily means fewer jobs.

These are comparisons of the matched occupations within each group, not every job in the economy. They show why exposure alone is insufficient to judge an occupation’s outlook. They cannot tell us how much employment would grow without AI, or how many workers AI might replace within a growing group.

Does broader task coverage mean greater risk?

The task-breadth view shows the same contrast: broad coverage can coexist with growth or decline. Switch the horizontal axis to task breadth. Business/finance and office support have nearly identical coverage, at 24.2% and 25.1%, but their growth projections remain +5.2% and −3.9%. Sales has somewhat narrower coverage (22.2%) and still declines, while computer/math has the broadest coverage of these four groups (34.1%) and the strongest growth (+10.0%).

For these groups, counting how widely AI appears across tasks does not resolve the difference in employment outlooks. Breadth counts tasks equally, regardless of their time requirements or importance, and does not distinguish assistance from replacement. Clicking a group and inspecting an occupation’s task panel helps readers see what lies behind its average, rather than treating all covered tasks as equivalent.

How might we explain the difference?

AI may complement professional skills while replacing routine tasks. This offers one possible explanation for the contrast between office support and computer/math occupations. Automating repetitive clerical tasks, such as data entry and document processing, could reduce demand for some office-support roles. Meanwhile, wider AI adoption could increase demand for workers who develop software, integrate AI systems, and evaluate their outputs. AI could also help these workers spend more time on design and complex problem-solving. Both groups contain varied tasks and skill requirements; the chart is consistent with this explanation, but does not establish that AI causes either projection.

Demand may offset labor savings: cheaper software or advice could attract more customers, supporting employment despite lower labor requirements per service. Trust, accountability, and unusual cases may preserve human roles, while adoption costs delay substitution. These mechanisms could explain divergent outlooks, but these chart comparisons do not establish them; BLS projections incorporate forces beyond AI.

Takeaway

A closer connection to AI does not consistently coincide with a weaker employment outlook. Business/finance and computer/math are projected to expand, while similarly exposed sales and office-support groups contract; switching to task breadth preserves this contrast. AI may save labor on some tasks while complementing skills or expanding demand elsewhere, so exposure need not translate directly into falling employment. For our own skill development and career choices, how AI penetrates specific tasks may matter more than whether a job is simply labeled “AI-exposed.” It is worth learning to use AI while strengthening judgment and interpersonal skills; exposure alone is not a reason to avoid an occupation.
