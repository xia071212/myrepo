Low Taxes, Deep Pockets? Who Funds Your State?

State Taxes
Public Finance
Tax Exporting
Do states that collect more tax really have richer governments, and who actually pays the bill?
Author

Yuting Xia

Published

October 19, 2026

Taxes vary widely across U.S. states and matter when choosing where to live. Higher taxes can support better public services, but do states that collect more tax really have richer governments? And what else decides how much money a state government has?

How far apart are state tax bills?

I use the U.S. Census Bureau’s Annual Survey of State and Local Government Finances, combining state and local governments because states divide responsibilities differently. Taxes per person is their combined tax revenue divided by population, averaged over FY2022–2024. It measures collections per resident, not each resident’s tax bill.

The range is wide. DC collects $14,967 per resident, compared with under $5,000 in Tennessee, Alabama and Mississippi. Higher collections cluster in the Northeast, California and Hawaii; lower collections extend across much of the South and interior West. Adjacent low-tax states form a broad southern belt rather than isolated exceptions. The pattern suggests regional differences in tax bases and fiscal choices, although the map alone cannot separate their effects.

Does counting all revenue close the gap?

Taxes are not a government’s only money. General revenue adds fees, tuition, interest, natural-resource royalties and federal transfers to taxes, excluding utility and pension-fund income. If these sources compensate low-tax states, the geographic gap should narrow.

General revenue ranges from $9,819 per person in Tennessee to $30,191 in DC. The gap barely narrows: the highest-to-lowest ratio remains 3.1, while the 90th-to-10th percentile ratio falls from 1.8 to 1.7.

On the map, the Southeast stays relatively light and the Northeast stays dark. The lower-revenue area stretches from Tennessee through Georgia and toward Florida, while California and Oregon remain comparatively well funded on the Pacific coast. Alaska, Wyoming and New Mexico stand out against this coastal pattern. Other income changes some states’ positions without erasing the broader regional divide. Darker colors everywhere partly reflect adding revenue sources; they do not by themselves show convergence.

The bars show why the gap persists: the ten highest-tax jurisdictions receive about $4,990 per person in federal transfers, versus $3,470 for the ten lowest. Federal transfers therefore do not offset the difference between these groups. This comparison describes funding patterns, not the causal effect of federal aid.

Alaska, Wyoming and New Mexico reach the top five in total revenue despite lower tax rankings. Their larger non-tax revenues raise another question: who actually pays the taxes they collect?

Who actually pays the taxes?

Taxes can fall on nonresidents, including visitors and customers buying goods produced elsewhere. I approximate their share by comparing Tax Foundation estimates of residents’ taxes paid to their own state with Census collections per resident in 2022. A 50% share means outsiders bear about half.

Connecticut’s −2% reflects differences between calendar-year estimates and fiscal-year collections, not an actual negative nonresident share.

The strongest nonresident shares lie in Alaska and a set of interior states: North Dakota, Wyoming and New Mexico. This is not a uniform western pattern: neighboring Idaho and Utah are blue, while Nevada is warmer. California and much of the Northeast also remain blue despite their relatively high tax collections. DC is a localized exception on the eastern seaboard. The geography of who pays differs from the geography of how much governments collect. Resource production offers one explanation for the interior hotspots; tourism and commuting offer others for Nevada and DC.

What the map tells us

Across the three maps, much of the Northeast combines high tax collections with high revenue, while parts of the Southeast remain lower on both. Alaska and selected interior states break this pattern, combining strong revenues with high estimated nonresident shares.

Higher collections generally accompany better-funded governments, but local tax bills do not tell the whole story. Where a state sits—and the economic activities connected to that location—helps shape both its funding and who bears the cost. These maps reveal associations, not proof that geography causes fiscal outcomes.

Amounts are nominal, without cost-of-living adjustments; nonresident shares combine modeled estimates and fiscal-year collections for one year.

Sources and replication. U.S. Census government finances, population estimates and state boundaries; Tax Foundation’s 2022 state-local tax burdens. Data, R scripts and replication instructions are in the GitHub repository and README.
